**Deliverable 2 — detailed report \| Evidence cutoff: 19 November 2026 \| Public sources**

**Overall judgement (medium confidence).** Australia has substantial and improving crisis governance, critical infrastructure obligations, prudential continuity rules, emergency warning capability and sector-specific plans. The public record nevertheless does not establish that essential services can maintain a defined minimum level **together** through a prolonged, compound disruption. The strongest evidence for that distinction is concrete: the 2026 Telstra timing fault reached emergency calling; a certificate failure disrupted parts of settlement; flood gauges and emergency communications failed in the Northern Rivers; and official reviewers identify shared digital suppliers and supply-chain nodes. These cases do not prove a general national incapacity. Nor do published frameworks, forecasts and response programs prove end-to-end recovery. The decision for 2027–29 is to convert existing duties and coordination into measured, independently checked service outcomes across sectors. ([CISC 2026, pp. 4–17](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [ANAO crisis audit, summary and paras. 3.68–3.105](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-government-crisis-management-framework), S16; [RBA operational-risk focus, §4.1](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32.)

## 1. The assessment separates rules, delivery and verified performance

**Question and boundary.** This report asks what Australia's public evidence establishes about national civilian preparedness and essential-service continuity at the cutoff, then uses that evidence to design eight stress scenarios for 2027–29. It covers national crisis coordination, critical infrastructure, telecommunications and emergency calls, financial services and payments, electricity, fuel and logistics, health, water, transport, warning and community preparedness. Defence strategy informs the threat setting, but military operational readiness is outside scope. The comparator section uses the United Kingdom and, where their distinct scope helps, official New Zealand, Canadian and Finnish frameworks. The aligned D3 catalogue holds 42 Australian primary sources (S01–S42), two UK comparator sources (S43–S44), the register analysis (S45) and 11 further Australian or operator sources (S46–S56) added at review; the three additional official framework sources are cited directly in Section 11 and listed in Appendix A as supplementary comparators. No international framework is treated as proof of implementation or a basis for ranking countries. The report does not treat secondary commentary as an authority.

**Fact** means a dated statutory text, documented incident, reported test result, official finding or stated decision. An official risk ranking or forecast remains a fact *about the issuing body's assessment*, not proof that the projected event will occur. **Judgement** means an evaluative inference from specified facts, including this report's assurance ratings. **Scenario** means a conditional stress path; its trigger, duration, geographic reach and combined consequences are assumed unless separately documented. Confidence rates the strength and fit of evidence for the stated claim: **high** for direct, precise primary records; **medium** for bounded synthesis across sources; **low** for a plausible inference with material unobserved links. Confidence is not event probability.

The inquiry method was to compare each claim with the D3 source catalogue and findings register, and to use the official documents and pinpoint locators assembled there. A source counts for the proposition it can actually support: a law proves a duty, an announcement proves a stated action or status, an audit proves its scoped assessment, an incident report proves its documented sequence, a scenario proves no occurrence, and an availability average does not prove survival of a specific shared-provider failure. Sources whose exact claims remained unverified were excluded or identified as provisional. The catalogue at Appendix A reproduces the D3 IDs, dates, direct URLs and locators, enabling line-by-line review. ([ANAO crisis audit, summary](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-government-crisis-management-framework), S16; [APRA CPS 230, paras. 27–60](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [RBA settlement incident, pp. 2–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33.)

**Release scope and method note.** This release comprises Deliverables 1 to 6 with the source catalogue (S01 to S56) and the findings register (F01 to F45), and it supersedes earlier drafts. The report was drafted with AI assistance. The author reviewed the claims and judgements, and cited sources were checked against the pages they link to, with any limit recorded in the source catalogue (Appendix A).

**Judgement (high confidence about the distinction).** The report scores *public assurance* rather than assigning an undisclosed national resilience grade. No public evidence identified in the reviewed sources of an asset-level national dependency graph, a comparable distribution of cross-sector recovery times, or a completed national exercise joining telecoms, payments, fuel and government does **not** establish that such work does not exist. Classified, supervisory, commercial and state records may contain it. This wording is essential: a search limit is a limit on what can be asserted, not evidence of absence. CISC explicitly calls for systemic assessment; CPS 230 requires entity-level mapping and testing; neither publication contains the proposed national outcome dataset. ([CISC 2026, pp. 4–7](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [CPS 230, paras. 27–47](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28.)

### Source hierarchy and known limits

An Australian official primary record is the preferred basis for material findings. This includes the Federal Register, independent public auditors, parliamentary reports, regulators, public market operators and government data. Operator accounts are valuable for their own systems but remain operator accounts; the Telstra external investigation was commissioned by Telstra, and ACMA's separate review must be distinguished. A pending court allegation is not a finding. Historical audits remain dated findings until later verification shows whether the defect persisted or was remediated. Formal response, funding, rule commencement, exercise and validated operational outcome are separate implementation stages. ([ACMA Telstra statement](https://www.acma.gov.au/acma-statement-telstra-outage), S11; [ACMA Optus court announcement](https://www.acma.gov.au/articles/2026-07/acma-takes-optus-mobile-court-over-september-2025-triple-zero-outage), S13; [ANAO Disaster Ready Fund audit, summary](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/award-of-funding-under-the-disaster-ready-fund), S21.)

Four items remain **provisional gaps** rather than report findings: the exact current implementation status of all Royal Commission natural-disaster recommendations; the full primary findings and government response to the 2026 parliamentary Triple Zero report; the precise status of the parliamentary national civil preparedness inquiry and its reported 2027 submission date; and final regulatory disposition and reconciled failed-call counts for the July 2026 Telstra outage. Each needs a dated official text before a definitive assertion. Secondary-source cloud-outage examples, unretrieved fuel FOI price figures and unverified ASIO cost estimates are omitted. ([Royal Commission report landing page](https://www.royalcommission.gov.au/natural-disasters/report); [ACMA Telstra statement](https://www.acma.gov.au/acma-statement-telstra-outage), S11.)

## 2. National governance is clearer than measured national readiness

**Fact (high confidence).** The Australian Government Crisis Management Framework is the Commonwealth capstone for near-term preparedness, response, relief and early recovery. It does not itself cover prevention, long-term recovery, reconstruction or risk reduction. PM&C's version history shows **AGCMF version 4.2, issued September 2026**, as current at the cutoff; versions 4.0 and 4.1 are superseded. Version 4.2 followed annual administrative revision, including coordination and exercise refinements. A revised framework is evidence of maintained governance, not validation of its performance under a multi-sector emergency. ([PM&C AGCMF framework, pp. 1, 10](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/agcmf-framework-v4.pdf), S18; [PM&C version control, v4.2 row](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/australian-government-crisis-management-framework-agcmf/version-control), S17.)

**Fact and judgement (high/medium confidence).** The 2024 ANAO audit judged PM&C's framework **largely appropriate** and identified a historical absence of a structured readiness assessment before the 2023 review. It found 32% of publicly available national plans had not been updated in the prior three years at July 2024 and that risk assessment did not then support selection of low-probability, high-consequence national exercise scenarios. The five audit recommendations were agreed. The proper 2026 conclusion is that the audit established a **2024 governance and exercise baseline**, while subsequent version revisions document process change; current performance against every recommendation has not been independently established here. A claim that all these gaps persisted in 2026 would overstate the audit. ([ANAO crisis audit, summary paras. 9–12 and paras. 3.68–3.105](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-government-crisis-management-framework), S16; [PM&C version control](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/australian-government-crisis-management-framework-agcmf/version-control), S17.)

The National Coordination Mechanism and higher AGCMF coordination tiers create a route for Commonwealth–jurisdictional response. They are important design assets. The 2024 COVID-19 Response Inquiry, however, shows why a mechanism's existence is insufficient: it recommended renewed national governance, more integrated health emergency planning, escalation and real-time review, with alignment to AGCMF. Those recommendations show lessons identified, while the reviewed source set does not verify completion of each action at November 2026. A pandemic inquiry also cannot be treated as a direct test of fuel, grid or telecom continuity. ([PM&C COVID inquiry, recommendations overview](https://www.pmc.gov.au/domestic-policy/commonwealth-government-covid-19-response-inquiry), S27; [PM&C AGCMF framework, p. 1](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/agcmf-framework-v4.pdf), S18.)

**Fact (high confidence).** The National Disaster Risk Reduction Framework explicitly calls for plausible future scenarios and attention to cross-sector dependencies. Its existence rebuts a claim that Australia lacks any national risk framework. The defensible question is whether decisions, exercises and independent measures now show that those dependencies have been reduced. NEMA's 2023–24 annual report rated milestones for its second National Action Plan achieved but the systemic monitoring, evaluation and learning baseline only **moderately achieved**. This is a self-reported implementation distinction, not a finding that the entire framework failed. ([National Disaster Risk Reduction Framework, p. 9](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/emergency/files/national-disaster-risk-reduction-framework.pdf), S26; [NEMA annual report 2023–24, performance measure 1.3](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-10/Annual%20Report%202023-24.pdf), S23.)

The 2020 Royal Commission concerned **natural disasters**. Its recommendations and later governance reviews matter for civil readiness, but must not be generalised into an audit of all non-natural hazards. The 2026 Disaster Recovery Funding Framework remained **proposed**, with emergency ministers still describing its development as ongoing on 5 November. ANAO found first- and second-round Disaster Ready Fund awards largely effective, yet project assessment only partly appropriate and the audit did not measure disaster-loss reduction. These counterposed facts show active reform and a remaining outcome-evaluation task. Similarly, ANAO's Defence assistance audit rated one old reporting recommendation partly implemented while recording three Defence-related Royal Commission recommendations implemented; it was not an audit of every national response operation. ([NEMA recovery funding proposal, 13 October 2026](https://www.nema.gov.au/our-work/disaster-recovery/disaster-recovery-funding-framework), S19; [NEMMM communiqué, 5 November 2026, pp. 1–2](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-11/Final%20Communique%20-%20NEMMM%205%20November%202026.pdf), S20; [ANAO Disaster Ready Fund audit, summary paras. 9–18](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/award-of-funding-under-the-disaster-ready-fund), S21; [ANAO Defence assistance audit, paras. 4.31–4.41](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/defence-assistance-to-the-civil-community), S22.)

**Judgement (medium confidence).** A national owner for cross-sector *outcomes* should be defined operationally: one body can convene, require a common test, receive results, arbitrate dependencies and escalate unresolved risks. The reviewed evidence does not permit a categorical statement that Australia has “no dedicated Commonwealth home” for all-hazard resilience. That formulation rests on secondary treatment of a natural-disaster governance review and is not used here. The practical gap is narrower and more useful: public reporting does not yet show which national body owns a measurable minimum-service result across simultaneous telecom, fuel, electricity, payments and emergency-management failures. ([ANAO crisis audit, governance and exercise findings](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-government-crisis-management-framework), S16; [CISC 2026, pp. 4–7](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01.)

## 3. Critical infrastructure rules have expanded; asset outcomes remain unevenly visible

The Security of Critical Infrastructure Act defines covered asset classes and obligations through statutory criteria. Its application to a particular bank, carrier, data centre or asset must be checked against the asset definition, responsible entity and applicable rules. CISC guidance distinguishes registration, critical-infrastructure risk management programs, cyber-incident reporting and data-service-provider duties. It reports enhanced CIRMP arrangements commenced **10 June 2026**, with differentiated categories. A significant-impact cyber incident can carry a **12-hour** SOCI notification clock and a relevant-impact incident **72 hours**; these are cyber-specific triggers, not general outage deadlines. ([SOCI Act, 4 June 2026 compilation, ss. 12F–12G and Part 2B](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2018A00029/2026-06-04/2026-06-04/text/original/pdf), S02; [CISC regulatory obligations, reporting and CIRMP sections](https://www.cisc.gov.au/how-we-support-industry/regulatory-obligations), S39.)

**Fact (high confidence).** The 2022 ANAO audit called Home Affairs' administration of critical-infrastructure protection policy **partly effective**, citing weaknesses in integrated risk documentation, engagement strategy, performance framework and approved compliance processes. This is a historical baseline. CISC's 2026 annual risk review reports updated rules, guidance and exercises, demonstrating subsequent policy activity but not, by itself, independently measured operating effectiveness for assets or sector networks. The direct ANAO analysis in the D3 source catalogue supports this assessment. ([ANAO critical-infrastructure audit, summary paras. 7–12](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/administration-critical-infrastructure-protection-policy), S03; [CISC 2026, p. 3](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01.)

**Official judgement (high confidence that CISC made it).** CISC's November 2026 review urges systemic rather than siloed risk assessment. Its figure ranks contracted-workforce insider risk highest for plausibility and pre-positioned cyber risk highest for possible damage. It discusses cloud and other common third parties, legacy IT/OT, AI implementation, compromised time and data integrity, single vendors and just-in-time supply. These are structured **qualitative assessments**. They are not numerical probabilities or proof that a particular attack, supplier failure or nationwide cascade will occur in 2027–29. The hospital and other cascade illustrations in the review are theoretical examples, not Australian incident records. ([CISC 2026, pp. 4–8, 10–17](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01.)

ASD's 2025–26 report records **154 critical-infrastructure incidents** in its response set. Its broader annual release reports more than 82,000 cybercrime reports and more than 1,500 incident responses across its remit. These figures demonstrate service demand and observed activity; changing reporting practice, detection and denominators preclude conversion into national attack prevalence or the probability of a simultaneous, cross-sector cyber event. CISC's threat ranking is better used to choose test cases than to assign likelihood labels. ([ASD critical-infrastructure chapter](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/annual-cyber-threat-report-2025-26/australian-cyber-threat-landscape/critical-infrastructure), S04; [ASD 2025–26 release, S56](https://www.asd.gov.au/news/2026-10-27-australian-signals-directorate-releases-seventh-annual-cyber-threat-report-2025-26).)

**Judgement (medium confidence).** The next assurance step is to ask each responsible operator for a bounded, testable statement: the exact critical service, geography and customers covered; maximum tolerated interruption; minimum degraded service; mapped internal, provider and fourth-party dependencies; backup power and fuel endurance; last exercised failover; and evidence of recovery and reconciliation. CISC's systemic lens gives the prioritisation, while CPS 230 offers a concrete model for regulated financial entities. A public national dashboard can aggregate outcomes without disclosing sensitive asset diagrams. ([CISC 2026, pp. 4–7](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [CPS 230, paras. 27–47](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28.)

## 4. Telecommunications incidents expose hidden critical functions

The **8 July 2026 Telstra outage** offers an unusually specific failure path. Technology Audit Partners' operator-commissioned external report traced planned timing-chassis work at about 2:50 am to a GPS card that emitted an incorrect 2006 date because a known rollover firmware update was absent. Earlier architecture and a later workaround allowed the bad time to propagate. Voice, messaging, data and some emergency calls were affected; most postpaid service returned by 12:12 pm. This report is evidence of the technical and governance sequence, subject to the commissioning and ACMA-status caveats. It does **not** substantiate named EFTPOS or regional-rail consequences, so those downstream edges are not asserted. ([Technology Audit Partners, PDF pp. 2–5](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [ACMA statement](https://www.acma.gov.au/acma-statement-telstra-outage), S11.)

Cross-reference to section 8. Different causes prevent any claim that one technical fault recurred, and this report makes none. The register analysis (S45, indicative only) shows a different common feature: the three most recent Australian national carrier outages with known causes (Optus November 2023, Optus September 2025 and Telstra July 2026) each trace to a change or upgrade failure, which points to change control and not to a shared technical fault.

The review found that timing was not classified as a critical “Sovereign Function”; ownership and end-to-end architecture were unclear; change pre-checks and post-change confirmation were weak; alarm visibility and specialist staffing were insufficient. Telstra accepted the findings and announced remediation. The improvement program is a real response, while completion and efficacy of each control require later evidence. The report also says recovery worked well once the cause was identified and incident command took over. It is therefore inaccurate to turn this case into a claim that all response systems failed. ([Technology Audit Partners, PDF pp. 5–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [Telstra response, 2 September 2026](https://www.telstra.com.au/exchange/what-we-ve-learned-from-the-external-investigation-of-our-july-o), S10.)

**Fact (high confidence).** ACMA's 2024 Optus enforcement statement says that during the 8 November 2023 outage, **2,145 people could not access Triple Zero** and **369 welfare checks were missed**; Optus subsidiaries paid more than \$12 million in penalties. This is a different incident and legal record from July 2026 Telstra. ACMA's July 2026 announcement of Federal Court proceedings over a September 2025 Optus outage alleges 1,005 emergency-call contraventions; no court liability is inferred. The fact of repeated emergency-call disruption warrants sector-level learning, while different causes prevent a claim that one technical fault recurred. ([ACMA Optus enforcement, November 2024](https://www.acma.gov.au/articles/2024-11/optus-pays-12-million-penalty-triple-zero-outage), S12; [ACMA Optus proceedings, July 2026](https://www.acma.gov.au/articles/2026-07/acma-takes-optus-mobile-court-over-september-2025-triple-zero-outage), S13.)

ACMA's customer-communication standard defines major-outage thresholds and requires regular multi-channel updates; emergency-call rules effective November 2025 address network-disconnect and camp-on behaviour, testing, information sharing and post-incident reporting. These are material regulatory additions. They do not guarantee bank data access, commercial mobile service continuity or handset reception during a power and backhaul failure. The relevant test is emergency-call **completion**, including alternative-network access and welfare handling, with independent timestamps and reconciliation of attempted versus connected calls. ([ACMA customer-outage guidance, “Defining” and “Requirements”](https://www.acma.gov.au/communicating-customers-during-major-and-significant-local-outages-guidance-and-faqs-industry), S37; [ACMA emergency-call rules](https://www.acma.gov.au/access-emergency-call-service-during-network-outages-faqs-industry), S38.)

**Unresolved fact check (low confidence).** The reviewed material does not reconcile competing July 2026 failed-call counts or establish ACMA's final regulatory finding. The final count should be taken from a dated regulator or custodian report defining denominator, duplicate calls and measurement window. Until then this report relies on the TAP finding that some emergency calls were affected, not an unqualified numerical total. ([Technology Audit Partners, PDF pp. 2–5](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [ACMA Telstra statement](https://www.acma.gov.au/acma-statement-telstra-outage), S11.)

## 5. Finance has detailed continuity duties and visible shared-provider exposure

**Fact (high confidence).** CPS 230 began on 1 July 2025, with targeted changes operative from 1 July 2026. For an authorised deposit-taking institution, payments, deposit-taking and management, custody, settlement and clearing are minimum critical operations unless the institution can justify exclusion. Boards must set tolerances for maximum interruption, maximum data loss and minimum service. Entities must identify people, technology, information, facilities, providers and interdependencies; maintain credible business continuity plans; exercise annually against severe but plausible scenarios, including provider disruption; and manage material providers and fourth parties. CPS 220 supplies the general risk framework, while CPS 190 concerns financial-viability recovery and orderly exit; these are related but distinct obligations. ([CPS 230, paras. 27–60](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [CPS 220, paras. 21–29](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-220), S30; [CPS 190, paras. 1–4 and 15 onward](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-190), S31.)

**Notification correction.** CPS 230 requires notification as soon as possible and within **24 hours** when a critical operation is disrupted beyond tolerance, and within **72 hours** for a qualifying material operational incident. Its service-agreement clock is **20 business days** after entering or materially changing the relevant agreement. A **10-business-day clock for BCP activation** is sometimes assumed; the standard does not support it and it is not used. CPS 234 has separate information-security notification clocks: 72 hours for qualifying incidents and 10 business days for certain material control weaknesses. A playbook must classify each trigger under the applicable regime, including any SOCI duty, and notify each regulator on time. ([CPS 230, paras. 32, 43, 60](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [CPS 234, paras. 35–36](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-234), S29; [CISC SOCI obligations](https://www.cisc.gov.au/how-we-support-industry/regulatory-obligations), S39.)

**Precise provider wording.** The RBA says **some of the largest regulated entities have around 150 service providers supporting critical operations** and **many providers serve multiple entities**, potentially across the industry. It does not say there are 150 shared providers across the whole Australian financial system, nor that every large bank has 150. Its central concern is concentration, low substitutability, offshore providers outside the domestic regulatory perimeter and opaque fourth/fifth-party chains. Entity-level CPS 230 compliance is therefore necessary but may leave correlated system exposure. ([RBA Financial Stability Review, October 2026, §4.1 “Concentration and lack of substitutability”](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32.)

The RBA reports an APRA **stress-test scenario**, not an observed outage, in which a material-provider interruption lasting three working days blocked debt-security settlement during liquidity stress. APRA’s own report says the outage lowered average bank Exchange Settlement balances by about \$11 billion more than the baseline, and that superannuation funds were only modestly affected. It was APRA’s first multi-industry test, with four major banks and six large superannuation funds. Banks could use existing Exchange Settlement balances but could not monetise some other high-quality liquid assets or access operations requiring securities settlement. That is a specific mechanism for a payments or settlement failure to become a liquidity problem. It should guide joint testing of service restoration, collateral movement, counterparties and communications, but cannot be cited as proof that an actual provider failure caused a banking liquidity event. ([RBA FSR, October 2026, §4.1 “System Risk Stress Test”](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32; APRA System Risk Stress Test final report, 30 June 2026, S46.)

**Observed counterpoint.** On **27 January 2026**, an expired intermediate database certificate disrupted RITS file transfer, LVCS/LVSS and property settlement; a replacement issued months earlier had not been installed. A separate duplicate-batch issue delayed further property settlements, while FSS settlement continued. The RBA identified monitoring, contact and contingency-playbook deficiencies and later reported procedural shortcomings across much of the assessment period. The incident proves a technical and governance dependency, but not the stylised liquidity cascade above. Conversely, the RBA reported 100% RITS and FSS availability for the September 2026 quarter, and its earlier retail study found quarterly average service availability of at least 99.8%. These high averages are genuine strengths and are still compatible with a severe individual incident. ([RBA incident report, pp. 2–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [RBA RITS assessment, §3.4 p. 12](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/self-assessments/2026/pdf/2026-assessment-rits.pdf), S34; [RBA availability statistics, table](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/), S35; [RBA retail reliability, “Availability”](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2024/oct/the-reliability-of-retail-payment-services.html), S36.)

**Judgement (medium confidence).** The finance sector has among the clearest published entity-level resilience obligations and regulator-led systemic analysis. Public evidence does not identify each institution's board-approved tolerances, independent failover results or cross-institution overlap by telecom, cloud and identity provider. That is a disclosure and assurance limit, not a finding that banks have failed CPS 230. The practical proof would be measured customer transactions, staff access, interbank messages, settlement backlog, data reconciliation and return to minimum service through a common-provider test. ([CPS 230, paras. 27–47](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [RBA FSR, §4.1](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32.)

## 6. Electricity, fuel, transport, water and health require distinct tests

**Electricity — fact and limitation (high confidence).** AEMO's August 2026 National Electricity Market outlook finds **no forecast reliability gap before 2030**, conditional on anticipated investment. It describes retirements, rising demand and data-centre load that increase the value of timely generation, storage and transmission. The June 2026 Western Australian outlook likewise expects sufficient South West Interconnected System capacity through 2028–29 under its assumptions. These forecasts weigh against an assertion of a baseline 2027–29 adequacy shortfall. They do not rule out a local flood, fire, operational or cyber outage, nor measure whether dependent telecom sites, hospitals, water plants and payment systems can operate through one. Gas forecasts concern defined regions and scenarios and should not be compressed into a national shortage prediction. ([AEMO NEM ESOO, 25 August 2026](https://www.aemo.com.au/newsroom/media-release/2026-ESOO), S05; [AEMO WEM outlook](https://www.aemo.com.au/newsroom/media-release/near-term-electricity-supply-outlook-improves-in-was-main-power-system), S40; [AEMO Gas Statement of Opportunities, executive summary, S54](https://www.aemo.com.au/-/media/files/gas/national_planning_and_forecasting/gsoo/2026/2026-gas-statement-of-opportunities.pdf).)

**Fuel — fact and limitation (high/medium confidence).** The March 2026 international supply shock prompted a Commonwealth Fuel Supply Taskforce, reserve and stockholding measures and a staged national response. It demonstrates real coordination and exposure to global supply chains. CISC's 2026 review says the Australian petroleum shock had **mainly cost effects** to date, a material counterweight to claims of national essential-service collapse. A target of 50 days of diesel or jet reserve is a policy target unless dated statistics verify achieved physical stocks. National stock does not equal regional pump availability, usable generator fuel or a guaranteed delivery route. The evidence does **not** establish that the fuel plan was literally written during the crisis; the report does not assert it. ([DCCEEW fuel response, March–June 2026](https://www.energy.gov.au/news/securing-australias-fuel-supply), S06; [CISC 2026, pp. 15–17](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [Australian Petroleum Statistics, data definitions, S55](https://www.energy.gov.au/energy-data/australian-petroleum-statistics).)

The Productivity Commission's 2021 study found most essential supply chains had proved resilient through pandemic and trade disruption and recommended targeted identification of vulnerable goods and nodes. That finding should restrain blanket assumptions of fragility. It also does not prove that every critical import or inland fuel-distribution route will withstand a different, longer shock. A defensible fuel test follows a named function—ambulance fleet, telecom generator, supermarket cold chain or water treatment—through supplier, depot, road access, refuelling and substitute, with actual regional quantities and lead times. ([Productivity Commission, \*Vulnerable Supply Chains\*, executive summary and chs. 4, 7](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/supply-chains/report/), S07; [DCCEEW fuel response](https://www.energy.gov.au/news/securing-australias-fuel-supply), S06.)

**Transport and flood warning — fact (high confidence).** The 2022 Northern Rivers study documented multiple flood- and rain-gauge failures. The NSW parliamentary inquiry identified inadequate emergency communications and flood vulnerability of tower support equipment and power. These records support a specific warning–communications–power pathway, while they do not establish that every gauge, tower and road failed together. Victorian audit work found public-transport operators prepared and responded under plans during the audited period, with coordination and passenger-impact improvements still needed. Its separate roads audit observed deterioration in road condition; this is relevant to access but does not by itself quantify emergency-delivery delay. ([NEMA Northern Rivers study, printed pp. 65–66](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/Characterisation%20of%20the%202022%20floods%20in%20the%20Northern%20Rivers%20region.pdf), S24; [NSW flood inquiry, para. 3.157](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/inquiries/2866/Report%20No%201%20-%20Response%20to%20major%20flooding%20across%20New%20South%20Wales%20in%202022.pdf), S25; [VAGO public-transport audit, key findings, S49](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/managing-disruptions-affecting-victorias-public-transport-network); [VAGO roads audit, key findings, S50](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/maintaining-state-roads?section=35655--1-our-key-findings&show-sections=1).)

**Water and health — fact and bounded inference.** NSW Health's drinking-water incident protocol requires prompt public-health notification when safety is threatened. FSANZ identifies flood-contact produce safety pathways. These sources establish possible service and health consequences, not national water or food scarcity. The 2024 IV-fluid shortage caused health ministers to coordinate supply and information, while the TGA's May 2026 update says acute shortages were resolved and modelling showed stable supply. That is direct positive outcome evidence. Future assurance should test alternative suppliers, hospital inventory, transport and electricity dependencies rather than carry the 2024 shortage forward as if still active. ([NSW Health drinking-water protocol, response criteria, S51](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/environment/water/Pages/nswhrp-microbiological.aspx); [FSANZ weather guidance, S52](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/food-safety-horticulture/weather-events); [Health Ministers, IV-fluid statement, pp. 1–2, S53](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/joint-statement-on-iv-fluids-health-ministers-meeting-16-august-2024.pdf); [TGA IV-fluids update, availability modelling](https://www.tga.gov.au/about-IV-fluids-shortages), S08.)

**Warning capability — fact and qualification.** NEMA reported its July 2026 AusAlert national test reached **{{ausalert.test_reach}} of mobile cells**, a preliminary cell-level result rather than proof that 94% of handsets received, understood or acted on an alert. NEMA now reports AusAlert operational, and the AusAlert site says it was ready for use from 1 October 2026 (S14, S48). The NEMA page carries no date, so 1 October 2026 is the date this report relies on. This is tangible implementation. Its performance through a prolonged carrier, grid or backhaul failure remains a separate test question, and effective warning still requires trusted content, reachable people and an action route. ([NEMA national test, preliminary results](https://www.nema.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/ausalert-national-test-success), S15; [NEMA operational announcement (undated page)](https://www.nema.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/ausalert-now-operational), S14; AusAlert site, S48.)

## 7. Dependency and assurance should be measured at the service edge

The central dependency problem is not simply that sectors depend on one another. It is that a single hidden function can cross organisational boundaries before any sector-level dashboard signals a shared risk. Telstra's network timing path is observed; the RBA's certificate-to-settlement path is observed; the Northern Rivers' gauge and communications weaknesses are observed. CISC's shared-cloud and single-vendor paths are official risk judgements. The RBA's common settlement-provider liquidity chain is a stress-test scenario. An electricity–telecom–warning–fuel cascade is a proposed compound test. Mixing these evidence classes would make the assessment appear stronger than it is. ([Technology Audit Partners, pp. 2–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [RBA incident report, pp. 2–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [CISC 2026, pp. 7, 10–17](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [RBA FSR, §4.1](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32.)

**Functional edge:** Carrier timing → mobile service → some emergency calls **Evidence status at cutoff: Fact, observed** in July 2026 ([TAP, pp. 2–5](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09) **What remains unproved:** Cross-carrier effects, reconciled failed-call count, named EFTPOS or rail harm

**Functional edge:** RITS certificate/file transfer → LVCS/LVSS and property settlement **Evidence status at cutoff: Fact, observed** in January 2026 ([RBA, pp. 2–6](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33) **What remains unproved:** A systemic liquidity incident; FSS continued

**Functional edge:** Flood gauges and exposed telecom support → warning/response weakness **Evidence status at cutoff: Fact, regional case** ([NEMA, pp. 65–66](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/Characterisation%20of%20the%202022%20floods%20in%20the%20Northern%20Rivers%20region.pdf), S24; [NSW inquiry, para. 3.157](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/inquiries/2866/Report%20No%201%20-%20Response%20to%20major%20flooding%20across%20New%20South%20Wales%20in%202022.pdf), S25) **What remains unproved:** National frequency or simultaneous failure of all paths

**Functional edge:** Shared cloud/identity → multiple critical operations **Evidence status at cutoff: Official judgement and regulated dependency** ([CISC, pp. 10–13](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [RBA, §4.1](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32) **What remains unproved:** Public provider overlap, substitution capacity, national recovery time

**Functional edge:** Fuel → freight, refuelling, backup generation **Evidence status at cutoff: Documented exposure; compound outage is scenario** ([DCCEEW fuel response](https://www.energy.gov.au/news/securing-australias-fuel-supply), S06) **What remains unproved:** Regional shortfall duration and service effects

**Functional edge:** Shared settlement provider → collateral and liquidity pressure **Evidence status at cutoff: APRA stress-test scenario** ([RBA, §4.1](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32) **What remains unproved:** Observed occurrence and external validity of magnitude

**Judgement (medium confidence).** A cross-sector test should begin with a public outcome sentence—for example, “during a 48-hour local power and carrier interruption, 90% of emergency calls in the test area complete by an independently measured alternative path, while ambulance dispatch and fuel replenishment continue at agreed minimum levels.” The numbers are **illustrative proposed acceptance criteria**, not current national standards. Each participant would then disclose enough to a trusted assessor to trace time, power, backhaul, fuel, identity, staff and supplier dependencies, including common fourth parties. The test would measure independent detection time, decision and notification time, degraded-service throughput, failed transactions, customer impact, data loss, restoration and backlog clearance. It would preserve failures and retest them, not only record a tabletop narrative. This design follows CISC's systemic framing and CPS 230's tolerance/test architecture. ([CISC 2026, pp. 4–7](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [CPS 230, paras. 27–47](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28.)

## 8. Implementation and counterevidence change the policy conclusion

### Status is a sequence, not a single label

For implementation tracking, this report uses six distinct states: **proposed**, **adopted or legally operative**, **funded or assigned**, **installed or delivered**, **tested under stated conditions**, and **independently validated against an outcome**. The states are not automatically sequential for every program, and one document can establish only some of them. The proposed Disaster Recovery Funding Framework remains at the design stage at the cutoff, according to NEMA and emergency ministers. CPS 230 is legally operative, but its publication cannot reveal a particular bank's exercise result. AusAlert is operational and has a national cell-level test result, but that does not establish handset receipt in all circumstances. Telstra has accepted remediation, but an acceptance statement is not an independent retest. AEMO's outlook is a conditional forecast, not an implementation milestone at all. This taxonomy prevents the common error of equating intent, rule and outcome. ([NEMA recovery funding proposal](https://www.nema.gov.au/our-work/disaster-recovery/disaster-recovery-funding-framework), S19; [NEMMM November communiqué](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-11/Final%20Communique%20-%20NEMMM%205%20November%202026.pdf), S20; [CPS 230](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [NEMA AusAlert test](https://www.nema.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/ausalert-national-test-success), S15; [Telstra remediation statement](https://www.telstra.com.au/exchange/what-we-ve-learned-from-the-external-investigation-of-our-july-o), S10; [AEMO ESOO](https://www.aemo.com.au/newsroom/media-release/2026-ESOO), S05.)

**Judgement (high confidence in the method).** A recommendation should be called “closed” only if the responsible entity can show the control is in operation and a repeatable test shows it works for the specified service. There is a second, harder question: did the control reduce loss in a real event? Many measures cannot be assessed on that endpoint quickly because severe events are rare and exposure changes. A combination of controlled exercise, incident performance and independent audit is therefore more informative than either an announcement or one benign season. ANAO's Disaster Ready Fund audit explicitly addressed award quality, not project effectiveness; the RBA settlement report addressed a real outage and documented corrective tasks; the TGA's IV-fluids update provides a genuine short-term outcome check. These three sources illustrate different points in the chain. ([ANAO Disaster Ready Fund audit, summary paras. 9–18](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/award-of-funding-under-the-disaster-ready-fund), S21; [RBA settlement report, pp. 6–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [TGA availability update](https://www.tga.gov.au/about-IV-fluids-shortages), S08.)

### Three incident lessons have different owners and remedies

The Telstra timing outage points to **operator engineering assurance**: identify sovereign or otherwise critical functions, assign an owner, control change, monitor continuously and retain enough expertise to restore service. The RBA settlement incident points to **market-infrastructure operations and participant coordination**: certificate lifecycle, file-transfer monitoring, duplicate-batch controls, current contingency contacts and clear member activation triggers. The Northern Rivers evidence points to **place-based multiagency assurance**: gauges, local site power, communications and access routes need to be tested in the same geography. A universal “improve resilience” action would obscure these distinct controls. A shared national exercise should connect them through service outcomes while each owner fixes its own edge. ([Technology Audit Partners, pp. 5–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [RBA settlement report, pp. 2–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [NEMA flood study, pp. 65–66](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/Characterisation%20of%20the%202022%20floods%20in%20the%20Northern%20Rivers%20region.pdf), S24; [NSW flood inquiry, para. 3.157](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/inquiries/2866/Report%20No%201%20-%20Response%20to%20major%20flooding%20across%20New%20South%20Wales%20in%202022.pdf), S25.)

**Judgement (medium confidence).** Change and software defects are the largest single cause of large outages in the evidence the assessment draws on, but they are not a majority cause. In a curated register of {{f152.outages}} publicly reported outages from 2016 to 2026, {{f152.changes}} ({{f152.share}}) trace to a software defect or a change that went wrong, nearly three times the next cause, power failure ({{f152.share_power}}). Excluding the four outages whose operators never disclosed a cause the share is {{f152.share_ex_unclassified}}, and it is {{f152.share_since_2022}} for events since 2022, but neither figure supports “most”. Two limits apply. Better-documented events skew toward this category, because change failures usually attract a published post-incident report: events with a primary or official source show {{f152.share_grade_b}} and events with secondary sources only show {{f152.share_grade_c}}, so the true share among all large outages could be lower. The register is also a sample of notable, widely reported events, not a census. The Australian cases fit the pattern. The Reserve Bank’s October 2022 payments outage came from an incorrect setting applied in a planned change, and the Telstra and Optus outages recur in the change category. This supports putting change control and engineering assurance beside redundancy in the tests above, and it is not a forecast of the next outage. (Global Outage and Cyber Breach Register, curated graded sample, {{f152.rows}} rows, 22 Nov 2026, S45. Two mixed-cause rows, NTT Docomo December 2022 and SoftBank November 2023, are typed hardware fault; counting both as change gives 44 of 90, or 49%. The Reserve Bank’s January 2026 settlement outage, caused by an expired certificate that the RBA says was not a change or upgrade, is typed as a process failure and not counted; counting it as a configuration error would give {{f152.sens_changes}} of {{f152.outages}}, or {{f152.sens_share}}.)

For the financial sector, a board's maximum disruption time needs to be compared with **customer-visible transaction completion**, not only restoration of a platform process. A restored payment gateway can still leave queued transactions, settlement exceptions, unavailable identity access or delayed reconciliation. CPS 230's tolerances cover time, data loss and minimum service precisely because those outcomes differ. The RBA's January incident shows why a contingency playbook should define who detects and declares an outage, how members learn the status of individual settlement streams, when they activate alternatives and how they establish a clean end state. This is an analytical test design, not a claim that every institution currently lacks those controls. ([CPS 230, paras. 34–47](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [RBA settlement report, pp. 6–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33.)

For telecommunications, an emergency-call measure should include **attempts, completion, retry and handoff**. Total call counts may hide users who tried repeatedly or could not place a call at all. An outage can also be detected by customer reports before a network alarm reaches an incident team; the Telstra report records that monitoring and change records affected detection and diagnosis. The July 2026 failed-call number remains a provisional gap because competing reports may use different windows and denominators. A national reporting format should define all of those fields before comparing incidents. This is a proposed method based on the documented July failure and ACMA's emergency-call obligations, not a new statutory requirement. ([Technology Audit Partners, pp. 2, 5–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [ACMA emergency-call guidance](https://www.acma.gov.au/access-emergency-call-service-during-network-outages-faqs-industry), S38.)

### Positive evidence should remain visible beside open risks

A credible national assessment must recognise working capacity. AEMO's conditional reliability outlook does not foresee a NEM adequacy gap before 2030, and its WA outlook expects sufficient SWIS capacity through 2028–29. RBA retail availability is high in aggregate, FSS continued during the January settlement incident, and the September RITS/FSS quarter was fully available by the reported measure. Telstra restored most postpaid service within the day and published an external root-cause review. AusAlert moved from national test to operational service; the TGA documented resolution of the acute IV-fluid shortage; and the Productivity Commission found many essential supply chains resilient during COVID disruption. These facts reject the claim that Australia is uniformly unprepared or incapable of recovery. ([AEMO NEM ESOO](https://www.aemo.com.au/newsroom/media-release/2026-ESOO), S05; [AEMO WEM outlook](https://www.aemo.com.au/newsroom/media-release/near-term-electricity-supply-outlook-improves-in-was-main-power-system), S40; [RBA availability statistics](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/), S35; [NEMA AusAlert launch](https://www.nema.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/ausalert-now-operational), S14; [TGA IV-fluid update](https://www.tga.gov.au/about-IV-fluids-shortages), S08; [Productivity Commission, executive summary](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/supply-chains/report/), S07.)

The same evidence resists overconfidence. A conditional adequacy forecast measures generation supply under assumptions, not resilience of a particular telecom site. An availability average can hide a severe outlier. A warning test covering cells is not verified handset delivery during network failure. An accepted recommendation is not a closed control, and funded mitigation is not reduced losses. The RBA's observed certificate event and Telstra's hidden timing dependency show why end-to-end test traces matter. This report's main judgement is therefore **documented implementation with uneven public assurance of compound outcomes**, rather than a binary “resilient/not resilient” verdict. ([RBA incident report](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [TAP, pp. 5–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [ANAO Disaster Ready Fund audit](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/award-of-funding-under-the-disaster-ready-fund), S21.)

## 9. Eight 2027–29 scenarios specify tests, not forecasts

The following are **scenarios**, not predicted events or official likelihood estimates. High, Moderate and Low likelihood labels would need calibrated base rates, a horizon denominator and an elicitation method, none of which is available, so none is used. Impact depends on duration, geography, coincidence, customer vulnerability and actual fallbacks. Confidence below measures the strength of the *mechanism* evidence, not probability. The 2025 National Climate Risk Assessment's 11 priorities and warming cases concern broader time horizons; they support compound-risk design but do not give 2027–29 event odds. AEMO's forecasts and APRA's technology outlook similarly supply conditional indicators, not a countdown to failure. ([DCCEEW National Climate Risk Assessment, introduction pp. 1–2](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/81097.pdf), S41; [AEMO NEM ESOO](https://www.aemo.com.au/newsroom/media-release/2026-ESOO), S05; [APRA System Risk Outlook, AI and quantum sections](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/apras-system-risk-outlook-november-2026), S42.)

### S1. A major carrier loses a hidden common service

**Scenario; mechanism confidence high, combined consequence confidence medium.** A timing, configuration or vendor change degrades one carrier's mobile voice/data and some emergency calls. The July 2026 Telstra report demonstrates the core mechanism, while Optus enforcement demonstrates a separate emergency-call failure history. The exercise should inject a wrong-time signal or similarly hidden shared function, then independently count attempted and completed emergency calls, camp-on to another network, public warning receipt, operator detection, ACMA communications and restoration. It should include priority users and a reconciliation of missed calls; payment or rail consequences should be added only where a named operator validates the edge. Indicators are unresolved timing ownership, change-control failures and failed emergency-call tests. **Decision trigger:** any material failure to meet pre-agreed emergency-call completion or restoration criteria requires a documented cross-carrier corrective action and repeat test. ([TAP, pp. 2–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [ACMA emergency-call rules](https://www.acma.gov.au/access-emergency-call-service-during-network-outages-faqs-industry), S38.)

### S2. A common cloud or identity service is unavailable for days

**Scenario; mechanism confidence medium, national reach confidence low.** A shared provider fault or compromised identity service prevents bank staff and customers, public agencies and health IT from using normal channels. CISC and RBA identify common-provider concentration; the exact overlapping customer set is not public in reviewed sources. Test degraded access without the primary identity plane, simultaneous provider communication loss, alternative transaction routes, manual approvals, data integrity and restoration sequencing. Monitor provider concentration, fourth-party discoveries and failover exercises, not news of one provider incident as a proxy for national impact. The proposed pass criterion is each critical operation remaining within its own CPS 230 tolerance and public-service minimum. No Australian multi-day cloud event is asserted. ([CISC 2026, pp. 10–13](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [RBA FSR, §4.1](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32; [CPS 230, paras. 34–47](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28.)

### S3. A prolonged international fuel shock reaches regional delivery

**Scenario; exposure confidence medium, outage severity confidence low.** An import or shipping disruption raises cost and constrains selected diesel deliveries. The 2026 response demonstrates policy machinery; CISC says Australian effects then were mainly cost effects. Stress a prolonged, geographically uneven case: depot stock, freight routes, backup generators, agriculture, ambulance and water utilities. Measure actual usable regional stock, delivery time, prioritisation rules and substitute routes rather than infer local supply from national days of cover. Indicators are official stock statistics, cargo arrival, regional shortages and government escalation. The test fails if an agreed priority service runs out of usable fuel before replenishment despite a national stock figure. ([DCCEEW fuel response](https://www.energy.gov.au/news/securing-australias-fuel-supply), S06; [CISC 2026, pp. 15–17](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [Australian Petroleum Statistics, S55](https://www.energy.gov.au/energy-data/australian-petroleum-statistics).)

### S4. Cyber disruption affects energy or telecom operations

**Scenario; threat basis medium, compound impact low.** A disruptive cyber operation targets an operator's IT/OT or outsourced support and coincides with strained communications. ASD response data and CISC's pre-positioning assessment justify the test, but this report does not claim an observed state-sponsored national sabotage event. Inject compromise detection, isolation, safe manual operation, restoration from trusted configuration and interagency notification under exact SOCI and other applicable clocks. Measure minimum service and data integrity while the incident is investigated. Watch verified official threat advice, control-test failures and provider access changes; do not infer event likelihood from ASD's incident count. ([ASD CI chapter](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/annual-cyber-threat-report-2025-26/australian-cyber-threat-landscape/critical-infrastructure), S04; [CISC 2026, pp. 5–7, 10–13](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [CISC obligations](https://www.cisc.gov.au/how-we-support-industry/regulatory-obligations), S39.)

### S5. Severe weather cuts local power, telecoms and warnings

**Scenario; component evidence high, joint extent medium.** Flood or fire damages grid and roads while backup at telecom and gauge sites degrades. Northern Rivers records establish component failures and NCRA supports compound-hazard analysis; AEMO's no-gap adequacy forecast does not preclude this local event. Exercise warning generation, cell broadcast and non-cell alternatives, emergency-call routing, site fuel and physical access, water safety and vulnerable-person welfare. Record who received an actionable warning and the time until minimum communications returned. Indicators are local asset exposure, backup duration, gauge redundancy and actual road access. ([NEMA flood study, pp. 65–66](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/Characterisation%20of%20the%202022%20floods%20in%20the%20Northern%20Rivers%20region.pdf), S24; [NSW inquiry, para. 3.157](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/inquiries/2866/Report%20No%201%20-%20Response%20to%20major%20flooding%20across%20New%20South%20Wales%20in%202022.pdf), S25; [NCRA](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/81097.pdf), S41; [AEMO NEM ESOO](https://www.aemo.com.au/newsroom/media-release/2026-ESOO), S05.)

### S6. Settlement infrastructure fails during liquidity stress

**Scenario; technical mechanism high, systemic outcome medium.** A certificate, file-transfer or shared-provider failure delays selected settlement just as institutions need collateral. The January RBA event proves one technical route, while APRA's stress test explores a separate common-provider liquidity route. A joint test should distinguish RITS, FSS and affected streams; measure alternate settlement, available Exchange Settlement balances, collateral mobilisation, member communication, backlog and duplicate detection. It should fail if restoration claims precede data reconciliation or if members cannot tell when to invoke contingency. Observed January impacts and stress-test consequences must remain separate in reporting. ([RBA incident report, pp. 2–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [RBA FSR, §4.1 stress test](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32.)

### S7. International connectivity is reduced at a cable or landing site

**Scenario; mechanism plausible, Australian concentration confidence low.** Damage or power failure at a landing site constrains international routes and exposes cloud, communications and time-sensitive services. CISC's broader digital-concentration assessment supports examining common paths, but the reviewed Australian public evidence does not quantify landing diversity, spare capacity, repair time or domestic service impact. An exercise should obtain those data confidentially, test rerouting and prioritisation, and report service outcomes in aggregate. A named likelihood tier is unjustified. The UK committee's subsea-cable discussion is only a comparator, never evidence that Australian assets share UK vulnerabilities. ([CISC 2026, pp. 7–13](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [House of Lords, communications recommendations](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld5902/ldselect/ldresilience/79/7904.htm), S43.)

### S8. Emergency-call reform creates transition risk

**Scenario; decision status low, service stakes high.** A future government decision changes the Emergency Call Person model or associated duties while existing networks and call routing remain live. The premise is conditional: a parliamentary recommendation is not an enacted reform, and its full primary report is one of the provisional verification gaps. Before any transition, require a dual-run plan, independently measured call completion, rollback authority, accessibility and regional tests, and a post-transition incident review. Monitor only official decisions, legislation and regulator guidance, not an assumed 2027 implementation date. The underlying need for uninterrupted service is established by ACMA's 2023 enforcement findings and current emergency-call obligations. ([ACMA Optus enforcement](https://www.acma.gov.au/articles/2024-11/optus-pays-12-million-penalty-triple-zero-outage), S12; [ACMA emergency-call rules](https://www.acma.gov.au/access-emergency-call-service-during-network-outages-faqs-industry), S38.)

## 10. The UK comparator offers governance choices, not an Australian diagnosis

The House of Lords National Resilience Committee's **16 November 2026** report criticised a cause-by-cause risk view for obscuring interconnected consequences. It recommended clearer central accountability, a national strategy, backup communications and regular tests. These are **committee recommendations**, not demonstrated UK policy or proof that Britain has solved the same problem. The UK Cabinet Office's 2025 response to its Covid-19 inquiry described a Cabinet committee and strengthened risk assessment; that is a government response with a different institutional context. Australia already has an AGCMF, NDRRF, NCM and sector regulators. The useful transfer is a design question: can a named convenor require consequence-based tests and publish whether essential-service minima were met? The answer must be established in Australian law, governance and exercise results, not inferred from UK criticism. ([House of Lords, summary and recommendations, 16 November 2026](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld5902/ldselect/ldresilience/79/7904.htm), S43; [UK Cabinet Office response, overview](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67879a232cca34bdaf58a239/UK_Government_Response_to_the_Covid-19_Inquiry_Module_1_Report.pdf), S44; [PM&C AGCMF](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/agcmf-framework-v4.pdf), S18; [NDRRF, p. 9](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/emergency/files/national-disaster-risk-reduction-framework.pdf), S26.)

## 11. New Zealand, Canada and Finland offer bounded framework comparisons

**Method (medium confidence).** These three official publications answer different design questions. New Zealand's strategy concerns disaster resilience and the relationship between national, regional, local and community action. Canada's first public profile is a staged hazard assessment. Finland's resolution is a broad governance model for vital societal functions. They are not like-for-like national performance measures. A comparison of their published architecture with Australia's National Disaster Risk Reduction Framework can identify choices for Australian testing, but cannot show which country would withstand a compound outage better. No audited, common cross-country outcome measure was identified in the reviewed sources. ([New Zealand NEMA, \*National Disaster Resilience Strategy\*, objectives](https://www.civildefence.govt.nz/strategy-capability/plans-strategies/ndrs); [Public Safety Canada, \*First Public Report of the National Risk Profile\*, Purpose and §9](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/2023-nrp-pnr/index-en.aspx/); [Finnish Government, \*Security Strategy for Society\*, pp. 2–3 and §2](https://julkaisut.valtioneuvosto.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/aff0ba13-4037-43e8-8b85-e138215ce3ac/content); [Australian NDRRF, p. 9](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/emergency/files/national-disaster-risk-reduction-framework.pdf), S26.)

**New Zealand — fact.** The *National Disaster Resilience Strategy* came into force on **10 April 2019** and runs to **2029**. Its objectives call for understanding hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity through scenarios; clarifying responsibilities across levels of government; improving information for emergency decisions; and assessing critical infrastructure capacity. The useful comparison for Australia is how a national framework connects community and local capability with infrastructure and government decisions. Australia also has a national disaster-risk framework that calls for plausible scenarios and cross-sector dependencies. The existence of either strategy says nothing by itself about the quality of local plans, the reach of public warning or measured recovery in an extended outage. The New Zealand disaster strategy is separate from its fuel-planning instruments; no fuel-plan review date is inferred from it. ([New Zealand NEMA, \*National Disaster Resilience Strategy\*, objectives 1, 9–12 and 16](https://www.civildefence.govt.nz/strategy-capability/plans-strategies/ndrs); [Australian NDRRF, p. 9](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/emergency/files/national-disaster-risk-reduction-framework.pdf), S26.)

**Canada — fact.** The first public *National Risk Profile*, dated **January 2024**, is an initial national strategic risk assessment of **earthquakes, wildland fire and floods**. It says later work will broaden coverage to other hazards and human-induced threats. Its value here is the transparent, staged method: specify the first hazard set and its limits, then expand coverage. It would be inaccurate to call the 2024 profile a completed all-hazards assessment or to infer tested Canadian continuity across telecommunications, fuel and payments. Australia could similarly publish the scope, uncertainty and future expansion of a service-consequence assessment while using its existing disaster-risk framework and AGCMF. That is a proposed method, not a finding that Canada has implemented a superior system. ([Public Safety Canada, \*First Public Report of the National Risk Profile\*, Purpose and §9](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/2023-nrp-pnr/index-en.aspx/); [Australian NDRRF, p. 9](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/emergency/files/national-disaster-risk-reduction-framework.pdf), S26; [PM&C AGCMF, pp. 1, 10](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/agcmf-framework-v4.pdf), S18.)

**Finland — fact.** The Finnish Government adopted the *Security Strategy for Society* on **16 January 2025**. The resolution describes interdependent vital functions and strategic tasks shared across authorities, businesses, organisations and citizens. Its broad societal scope makes it a governance comparator for the cross-sector outcome ownership discussed in this report. It does not demonstrate that every Finnish function has been independently stress-tested or that a Finnish model can be transplanted without adapting Australia's federal and regulatory arrangements. The practical Australian question is whether existing PM&C, NEMA, CISC, sector regulators, jurisdictions and operators can agree who measures a vital service end to end and who resolves a failed test. The Finnish publication supplies a way to frame that question; Australian evidence must answer it. ([Finnish Government, \*Security Strategy for Society\*, Government Resolution 2025:3, pp. 2–3 and §§2, 4](https://julkaisut.valtioneuvosto.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/aff0ba13-4037-43e8-8b85-e138215ce3ac/content); [Finnish Government adoption notice, 16 January 2025](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/236553176/finland-s-comprehensive-security-reinforced-through-new-security-strategy-for-society); [PM&C AGCMF, pp. 1, 10](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/agcmf-framework-v4.pdf), S18; [CISC 2026, pp. 4–7](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01.)

**Official framework:** New Zealand, 2019–29 **Documented scope:** Disaster resilience across government, infrastructure and communities ([NEMA strategy, objectives](https://www.civildefence.govt.nz/strategy-capability/plans-strategies/ndrs)) **Useful Australian design question:** Are local capacity, community action and critical-infrastructure tests joined to national service outcomes? **Limit:** Strategy publication does not prove delivery.

**Official framework:** Canada, January 2024 **Documented scope:** Initial profile of earthquakes, wildfire and floods ([Public Safety Canada, Purpose and §9](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/2023-nrp-pnr/index-en.aspx/)) **Useful Australian design question:** Can a national public assessment state its hazard coverage, uncertainty and expansion path precisely? **Limit:** Initial profile is not a complete all-hazards assessment.

**Official framework:** Finland, January 2025 **Documented scope:** Interdependent vital functions and strategic tasks ([Government Resolution 2025:3, pp. 2–3 and §2](https://julkaisut.valtioneuvosto.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/aff0ba13-4037-43e8-8b85-e138215ce3ac/content)) **Useful Australian design question:** Who owns and tests a vital function across authorities and private operators? **Limit:** Governance model does not establish tested performance.

**Judgement (medium confidence).** The most useful lesson from all four comparators, including the UK discussion above, is methodological: distinguish a published risk or governance framework from a measured consequence, then test the latter in its own institutional setting. Australia's NDRRF already names dependencies, and the AGCMF already provides crisis coordination. The report's recommendations therefore focus on accountable minimum-service outcomes and evidence of performance, rather than importing a foreign title or treating any comparator as a national score. ([Australian NDRRF, p. 9](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/emergency/files/national-disaster-risk-reduction-framework.pdf), S26; [PM&C AGCMF, pp. 1, 10](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/agcmf-framework-v4.pdf), S18; [House of Lords, recommendations](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld5902/ldselect/ldresilience/79/7904.htm), S43.)

## 12. A sequenced assurance program can make resilience reviewable

**Recommendation 1 — define national minimum-service outcomes (priority: immediate; lead: PM&C/NEMA with states, regulators and operators).** Select a small set of functions—Triple Zero, public warning, retail payments, critical settlement, fuel for emergency fleets and backup generation, safe drinking water and acute health logistics. For each, agree a target service population, maximum interruption, minimum degraded throughput, data-loss limit and escalation route. Publish the definitions and aggregate results; retain sensitive asset and supplier details in protected channels. This adapts CPS 230's tolerance logic to cross-sector outcomes without pretending its prudential standard legally governs every operator. The first deliverable is a signed outcome register with accountable convenor and participating entities, not another general resilience statement. ([CPS 230, paras. 34–47](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [AGCMF, pp. 1, 10](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/resource/download/agcmf-framework-v4.pdf), S18; [CISC 2026, pp. 4–7](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01.)

**Recommendation 2 — map common dependencies under trusted access (priority: first 12 months; lead: CISC with APRA, RBA, ACMA and operators).** Build a service-to-provider map that includes timing, identity, cloud, backhaul, electricity, fuel, workforce, critical software and fourth parties. Require each edge to state tested alternative capacity and recovery time. A public summary should report concentrations and unresolved single points without exposing exploitably precise site detail. Prioritise edges documented by incidents, then those CISC and RBA identify as shared. A valid map should be versioned after significant supplier changes and incidents. ([CISC 2026, pp. 7, 10–17](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [RBA FSR, §4.1](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/focus-topic-operational-risk-and-financial-stability.html), S32; [CPS 230, paras. 27–31, 48–60](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28.)

**Recommendation 3 — run an annual compound exercise with independent measurement (priority: begin within 12 months; lead: NEMA/PM&C with state controllers and sector regulators).** Start with S1/S5 because they contain observed component failures, then join S2/S6 and S3. Exercise actual degraded service and restoration, including public communication and vulnerable-user outcomes. Use third-party monitors where feasible, preserve transaction and call traces, and publish a bounded after-action report: intended minimum, achieved minimum, failures, owner, closure date and repeat-test result. The first exercise should explicitly document its hazard-selection rationale, addressing the historical ANAO finding. ([ANAO crisis audit, paras. 3.93–3.105](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-government-crisis-management-framework), S16; [TAP, pp. 5–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [RBA incident report, pp. 6–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33.)

**Recommendation 4 — verify incident remediation and regulatory handoffs (priority: ongoing; leads: each operator and regulator).** For Telstra timing, confirm function ownership, firmware inventory, change pre-checks, 24-hour alarms, staffing depth and actual re-test. For emergency calls, reconcile failed-call datasets and test alternative-network paths under load. For RITS, verify certificate lifecycle, duplicate detection, member contacts and backlog reconciliation. Establish a trigger matrix for CPS 230, CPS 234, SOCI and ACMA; train legal entities against the correct clocks. Close an item only on evidence of implementation and performance, not acceptance of a recommendation. ([TAP, pp. 5–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [RBA incident report, pp. 6–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [CPS 230, paras. 32, 43, 60](https://www.apra.gov.au/standards/cps-230), S28; [CISC obligations](https://www.cisc.gov.au/how-we-support-industry/regulatory-obligations), S39.)

**Recommendation 5 — attach evaluation to recovery funding and warning rollout (priority: 12–24 months; leads: NEMA, jurisdictions and funded operators).** For Disaster Ready Fund projects and any replacement recovery-funding framework, record baseline risk, completed work, exposure addressed and post-event or simulated service outcomes. For AusAlert, measure handset receipt, timeliness, comprehension and response in controlled tests that include degraded connectivity. Report the denominator and uncertainty. This turns a program or preliminary test measure into useful assurance without dismissing the real implementation already achieved. ([ANAO Disaster Ready Fund audit, summary paras. 9–18](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/award-of-funding-under-the-disaster-ready-fund), S21; [NEMA national test](https://www.nema.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/ausalert-national-test-success), S15; [NEMA operational launch](https://www.nema.gov.au/about-us/media-centre/ausalert-now-operational), S14.)

**Recommendation 6 — maintain a public evidence ledger (priority: immediate; lead: PM&C/NEMA with regulators).** Each major national claim should name the source and date, whether it is a requirement, plan, delivery record, test or outcome, the responsible entity, unresolved counterevidence, next verification point and confidence. D3 provides a starting source/finding register; the ledger should update without silently carrying historical weaknesses into the present. A published annual summary should state where no public evidence was identified in a defined source set. This is especially important for the four provisional gaps and for changes to forecasts and policy status. ([ANAO crisis audit, summary](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-government-crisis-management-framework), S16; [NEMMM communiqué, 5 November 2026](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-11/Final%20Communique%20-%20NEMMM%205%20November%202026.pdf), S20.)

### What would change this judgement

The overall “uneven public assurance” judgement would strengthen substantially if an authorised national body published a dated inventory of critical functions and common dependencies, measured pass/fail results for compound exercises, independent validation of degraded-service and recovery performance, closure evidence for major incident controls, and repeatable community-outcome measures. It would worsen if later official incident reports found repeated failure of the same control after documented closure, if material common providers lacked tested substitutes, or if prolonged regional fuel and telecom interruption defeated priority service minima. These are **prospective verification criteria**, not claims that the evidence already exists or that a future failure will occur. ([CISC 2026, pp. 4–7](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [ANAO crisis audit, paras. 3.93–3.105](https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/australian-government-crisis-management-framework), S16.)

## Conclusion

Australia's public evidence supports a more precise national priority than another general declaration of preparedness: set minimum essential-service outcomes, identify the few shared functions that can defeat them, and prove performance through compound tests with independent measurement. This builds on functioning governance, sector rules, warning rollout and documented recovery; it also answers the limitation exposed by the Telstra, RITS and flood cases. The 2027–29 scenarios are useful only if they produce evidence about actual service continuity and closed weaknesses, rather than becoming uncalibrated forecasts. ([CISC 2026, pp. 4–17](https://www.cisc.gov.au/resources-subsite/Documents/critical-infrastructure-annual-risk-review-2026.pdf), S01; [TAP, pp. 2–10](https://www.telstra.com.au/content/dam/tcom/dynamic-media-projects/luke-campbell/TAPFindingsforTelstraOutage.pdf), S09; [RBA incident report, pp. 2–7](https://www.rba.gov.au/payments-and-infrastructure/rits/system-availability-statistics/2026/pdf/payments-settlement-outage-20260127.pdf), S33; [NEMA flood study, pp. 65–66](https://www.nema.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/Characterisation%20of%20the%202022%20floods%20in%20the%20Northern%20Rivers%20region.pdf), S24.)
